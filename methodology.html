Methodology

Draft. Covers what is implemented so far: Kuhn poker, Leduc poker and Liar's Dice, direct and play modes, and the reporting site.

What is measured

StrategyBench measures how much value a best-responding opponent could extract from an AI subject's behaviour in a small two-player zero-sum card or dice game, compared with equilibrium play.

Results describe the model plus this prompt plus these sampling settings on this game. They are not a measure of general game-playing or reasoning ability.

Setup

  • Games are OpenSpiel games. Seat 0 is "Player 1" in prompts.
  • An information state (infostate) is what the acting player knows: their private card(s) or die and the public action history.
  • A behavioural strategy gives a probability distribution over legal actions at each infostate.
  • The equilibrium reference is the average policy of OpenSpiel's C++ CFR+ after 2,000 iterations, saved to disk so every run of a game is scored against the same reference.
Game Decision infostates Player 1 value Reference exploitability
Kuhn poker (kuhn_poker) 12 −0.05556 (theory −1/18) 4.4e-5
Leduc poker (leduc_poker(suit_isomorphism=True)) 288 −0.0856 8.5e-5
Liar's Dice (liars_dice(numdice=1,dice_sides=3)) 192 (96 forced) +0.1111 1.2e-6

(Values printed by scripts/run_benchmark.py as "equilibrium seat values"; the reference exploitability is equilibrium.exploitability in every run's metrics.json. Infostate counts are also printed by scripts/game_size.py.)

Leduc uses OpenSpiel's suit-isomorphic variant. The prompt only ever names ranks (suits do not matter in Leduc), so two infostates that differ only by suit would get identical prompts. The suit-isomorphic game deals ranks with the correct probabilities, so its infostates are exactly what the subject can distinguish: 288 instead of 936, with the same game value.

Liar's Dice is OpenSpiel's version with one three-sided die per player. Each player rolls a die showing 1, 2 or 3 and sees only their own. Player 1 must open with a bid; then the players alternate, each either bidding higher or calling LIAR. A bid QxF claims that at least Q of the two dice show face F. The highest face, 3, is wild: it counts towards a bid on 1 or 2, and a bid on 3 counts only 3s. Bids rank by quantity, then face (1x1 < 1x2 < 1x3 < 2x1 < 2x2 < 2x3). When LIAR is called the bidder wins if the bid is true and the caller wins otherwise, for 1 point. This is not the common six-sided game with 1s wild; the prompt states OpenSpiel's rules in full, checked against the installed OpenSpiel source and by tests that score challenges.

Half of its 192 infostates are forced: after the highest bid, 2x3, only LIAR is legal. Direct mode does not query forced decisions in any game (see "Querying the subject"), so a run asks about 96 infostates (19,200 calls at N = 200). Three sides was chosen to keep real-model runs affordable: four sides has 1,024 infostates (512 forced) and six has 24,576 (12,288 forced) (scripts/game_size.py).

Querying the subject

The system prompt states the rules (versioned, e.g. kuhn-v1). The user message describes the seat, the private card or die, the action history so far and an enumerated list of legal action labels, and asks for exactly one label. Both seats use the same template.

Parsing is strict. After removing surrounding whitespace, quotes, backticks, asterisks and one trailing period, and ignoring case, the reply must equal exactly one legal label. A reply that names a real game action which is not legal at that point is counted as illegal; anything else is a parse failure. A failed attempt is retried with the same prompt up to 2 times (by default). Every attempt is counted. A sample that never yields a legal action is recorded as failed; no action is ever substituted.

Estimating the strategy (direct mode)

Every decision infostate is enumerated with OpenSpiel and the subject is queried there independently, with a fresh context each time.

  • Text subjects are sampled N times per infostate. The estimated strategy σ̂ at an infostate is the empirical frequency of the valid sampled actions.
  • Distribution subjects (models that return a probability per offered option in one call) are queried K times per infostate and σ̂ there is the average of the returned probabilities. A subject whose answer never changes (the scripted references) is queried once and has no sampling error. Jev's answers vary between calls (by up to about 0.09), so it is queried K = 20 times and bootstrapped over its replies, like a text subject over its samples. A reply is valid when it names exactly the legal options with non-negative probabilities summing to 1 within 0.015; Jev rounds to two decimals, so three options can sum to 0.99, and such replies are rescaled to sum to 1 and counted (rescaled in the reliability block). Their probabilities may be intended as confidences rather than as a mixed strategy, so results report which reading was used.

Forced decisions are not queried. An infostate with a single legal action has nothing to estimate, so the subject is not asked: the action is played with certainty, the infostate is reported as forced (never as filled), and it counts as covered. M1 to M3 are unchanged by this, because the action there is certain either way; M4 only counts decisions that were asked. Kuhn and Leduc have no forced decisions; half of Liar's Dice's infostates are forced. Play mode still asks the subject at forced decisions.

Fill rule. An infostate with fewer than min_samples valid samples (default 1) is played uniformly at random over its legal actions. The number of filled infostates is always reported.

Validating by play (play mode)

The subject plays complete hands against a fixed opponent (the equilibrium, or uniform random). Seats alternate every hand. Chance and the opponent are sampled from a per-hand random generator seeded by the run seed, so a match is reproducible. Each subject decision is a fresh query with its own sample key, independent of the direct-mode samples. If every attempt at a decision fails, the subject's action is drawn uniformly from the legal actions and the decision is counted as a fallback; the number of fallbacks is reported.

Cross-check. After a direct run, the exact expected payoff of the estimated strategy σ̂ against the opponent, per seat, is compared with the play-mode mean. They are reported as agreeing when the difference is within 1.96 combined standard errors (the play-mode standard error, plus the bootstrap standard error of the exact value). This checks that σ̂ predicts how the subject actually plays.

Example (mock subject playing 80% equilibrium and 20% uniform, with 5% unparseable replies, 200 samples per infostate, 20,000 hands against the equilibrium, Leduc):

Seat Exact (from σ̂) Simulated z
Player 1 −0.2126 −0.2179 −0.14
Player 2 −0.0246 −0.0267 −0.06

(Produced by scripts/run_benchmark.py --game leduc --agent cfr-noisy --samples 200 --mode both --hands 20000 --garbage-rate 0.05 --format-noise.) With 10,000 hands per seat the 95% interval is about ±0.075 chips per hand, so play mode is far more expensive per unit of precision than direct mode, and is used as a check rather than as the headline measurement.

Metrics

All values are expected payoffs per hand (per round in Liar's Dice), reported in native units and normalised by the game's unit (1 ante in Kuhn and Leduc, 1 point in Liar's Dice, so the two coincide).

Definition
M1 exploitability OpenSpiel exploitability(game, σ̂) = NashConv(σ̂) / 2. In a two-player zero-sum game this is the average, over the two seats, of what a best responder gains against σ̂ beyond the equilibrium value.
M2 best-response gain, seat i With the subject in seat i: value of a best response in the other seat against σ̂, minus that seat's equilibrium value. M1 is the mean of the two M2 values (checked by a test).
M3 head-to-head loss, seat i Equilibrium value of seat i minus the subject's exact expected payoff in seat i against the equilibrium strategy.
M4 reliability Parse-failure rate and illegal-action rate (per attempt), retry rate and sample-failure rate (per sample), and coverage: the reach-weighted fraction of infostates with at least k valid samples (default k = 5), with reach measured under σ̂.

M3 is weaker than M1. Against an equilibrium opponent, any strategy that only uses actions in the equilibrium's support earns exactly the equilibrium value. In Kuhn poker a subject that plays the right actions with the wrong frequencies therefore has M3 ≈ 0 while still being exploitable (M1 > 0). M1 is the headline metric for this reason.

Where a best responder gains

Each run page splits the best-response gain M2 into the situations where it is earned. Fix the subject's estimated strategy σ̂ in seat i and let the other seat j switch from its equilibrium strategy σ* to a best response BR against σ̂. Then, exactly,

M2_i  =  M3_i  +  Σ over j's infostates I of  contribution(I)

contribution(I)  =  reach*(I) · [ q(I, BR(I)) − Σ_a σ*(I, a) · q(I, a) ]
  • reach*(I) is the probability that I is reached when seat j plays the equilibrium against σ̂ (chance × σ̂ × σ*).
  • q(I, a) is the expected payoff to seat j of taking action a at I, given that I was reached, when j best-responds from then on. It is computed from OpenSpiel's BestResponsePolicy, whose counterfactual values are divided by the infostate's counterfactual reach.
  • The bracket is the per-visit edge of the best response over the equilibrium mix. It is never negative, because the best response maximises q(I, ·).

So M3 is what the equilibrium wins without adapting, and each contribution is how often a situation arises times how much more adapting earns there. The identity is a telescoping (performance-difference) argument over seat j's decisions; the tests check it to 1e-9 on Kuhn and Leduc for several strategies, and against hand-computed Kuhn values (for an always-check/fold Player 1, Player 2's situations "Jack after a check" and "Queen after a check" contribute 4/9 and 1/3).

The page shows the situations with the largest contributions, with a templated sentence for each, and the subject's own action frequencies at every infostate against the equilibrium, ordered by reach (opponent at equilibrium) times total variation distance from the equilibrium.

Prompt sensitivity

Two optional variants measure how much results depend on wording (--prompt):

  • <game>-v1-para: the same rules, reworded and reordered.
  • <version>+shuffled: the legal actions listed in a shuffled order, fixed per infostate.

The spread of a model's metrics across these variants is reported alongside its main result.

Uncertainty

Each bootstrap replicate (200 by default) resamples the valid samples at every infostate with replacement, keeping that infostate's sample size, and recomputes every metric.

Plug-in bias. Exploitability and the best-response gains are maxima over opponent strategies. For any fixed opponent strategy the subject's estimated payoff is unbiased, but the best responder picks its strategy using the same noisy estimate, so sampling noise is exploited and never cancels out: the exploitability of the estimated strategy (the plug-in estimate) is biased upwards. The bias is largest near equilibrium, where the best responder is indifferent almost everywhere, and the bootstrap replicates are biased upwards again, so a percentile interval (the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the replicates) sits above the truth.

What runs report. For every metric (M1, and M2 and M3 per seat):

  • the bias-corrected estimate max(0, 2·x − mean of replicates), where x is the plug-in value;
  • the basic bootstrap interval [2·x − q97.5, 2·x − q2.5], clipped at 0, which is centred on the bias-corrected estimate.

The site shows these, with the plug-in value beside them. metrics.json also keeps the percentile intervals (ci95_percentile) and the bootstrap bias. Exact subjects (distribution clients queried once) have no sampling error and are shown exactly.

Coverage, measured by simulation. For scripted strategies with a known exploitability, scripts/ci_coverage.py draws independent datasets of N = 200 samples per infostate, builds both intervals for each, and counts how often they contain the true value:

Kuhn equilibrium 0.000 0.014 (0.006) 0.008 0% 88%
Kuhn 80% equilibrium, 20% uniform 0.066 0.070 (0.011) 0.067 94% 94%

(Kuhn: 200 datasets with 200 replicates each, scripts/ci_coverage.py --game kuhn. Leduc: 60 datasets with 100 replicates each, scripts/ci_coverage.py --game leduc --datasets 60 --bootstrap 100; with 60 datasets each coverage figure is uncertain by about ±4 points.)

What this shows:

  • The bias-corrected estimate is closer to the truth than the plug-in estimate in every case, but near equilibrium only part of the bias is removed (Leduc: 0.062 against a true 0.000).
  • Percentile intervals never covered an equilibrium subject's true value in either game. Basic intervals did so 88% of the time in Kuhn but only 30% in Leduc.
  • Away from equilibrium both intervals are close to nominal in Kuhn and somewhat below it in Leduc; for Leduc's 80/20 mix the basic interval did worse than the percentile one.

So the reported intervals are approximate: reasonable for clearly exploitable subjects, optimistic for subjects close to equilibrium, especially in the larger game. A maximum is not differentiable at an equilibrium, which is a known limit of the bootstrap. More samples per infostate is the reliable remedy. A cross-fitted estimate (best response chosen on half the samples, evaluated on the other half), which is biased downwards and so brackets the truth with the plug-in value, is a candidate for a later version.

Validation

The pipeline is checked against known values (see tests/):

  • Kuhn equilibrium value −1/18 for Player 1.
  • Always-pass: exploitability 1, M2 = 17/18 and 19/18, M3 (seat 1) = 1/6.
  • Always-bet: exploitability 1/3, M3 (seat 1) = 1/18.
  • Uniform random: exploitability 11/24, both by direct computation and through the full text pipeline.
  • An equilibrium-playing subject through the pipeline: < 1e-3 when probabilities are read directly, < 0.01 when sampled 5,000 times per infostate.
  • Mock subjects with injected malformed and illegal output: measured failure rates match the injected rates.
  • Rerunning from the cache reproduces every result with zero calls.
  • Leduc: deterministic subjects through the full pipeline match OpenSpiel's direct computation exactly; uniform and equilibrium subjects read as distributions match OpenSpiel to 1e-9 and < 1e-3 respectively.
  • Liar's Dice: challenges are scored as the rules text says (wild 3s, bids on 3, bidder and caller wins); every history in an infostate renders the same prompt, so the opponent's die is never shown; deterministic subjects through the full pipeline match OpenSpiel's direct computation exactly; uniform and equilibrium subjects read as distributions match OpenSpiel to 1e-9 and < 1e-3. Always the lowest legal bid has exploitability 7/9 and always calling LIAR 8/9, both hand-computed (the best responder against the lowest bid bids 2x2, forcing the subject to 2x3, and calls LIAR).
  • Play mode: always-pass as Player 1 in Kuhn scores −2/9 against the equilibrium within the simulation's 95% interval; matches are reproducible from the seed.
  • Breakdown: M2 = M3 + sum of contributions to 1e-9 on Kuhn and Leduc, and hand-computed per-infostate contributions for always-pass in both Kuhn seats.
  • Site: every number on every page is re-read from its result file at build time; tests check that an edited number or an edited published file is caught.

Reproducibility

Every attempt is logged to calls.jsonl with the model, parameters, prompt version and hash, the user prompt, raw output, parse result, token counts, latency and timestamp. The system prompt is constant within a run and is stored once in manifest.json. Replies are cached on (provider, model, parameters, prompt hash, sample index, attempt), so reruns are free and identical.

Reporting

scripts/build_site.py builds the static site from the results directory in one command.

Which runs are shown. Every run directory with manifest.json and metrics.json is a candidate. Sampled runs with fewer than 30 valid samples requested per infostate are smoke tests and are left out. Runs that measure the same thing (game, provider, model, parameters, prompt version and estimation kind) are deduplicated, keeping the most recently created. Runs can also be excluded by name. The build lists every run it leaves out, with the reason, in its output and in build.json.

Reference subjects. Rows marked reference are scripted policies, not LLMs, answering through the same text interface (scripts/run_references.py): the equilibrium and uniform random (each read exactly, and sampled at N = 200 like a model), always the first legal action (check or fold in poker; the lowest legal bid in Liar's Dice), always the last legal action (the most aggressive action in poker; in Liar's Dice, calling LIAR whenever it is legal, which means opening with 2x3), and an 80/20 mix of equilibrium and uniform with 5% malformed replies. They anchor the scale and keep the pipeline honest against known values. The exact rows give the true values; the sampled rows show what a run of the same size reports for the same strategy, sampling noise included. One such draw is visibly unlucky: Kuhn's sampled uniform subject lands well above the exact 11/24 because the infostate where Player 2 holds the King and faces a bet happened to draw 61% folds. It is shown as drawn.

Game wording. Pages use each game's own words, supplied by its GameSpec: rounds instead of hands in Liar's Dice, and names for the scripted references in the game's terms.

Prompt variants (-para, +shuffled) are listed in their own table; the main table shows each subject's range of exploitability across the prompts it was run with.

Replays. The replay view steps through a hand, showing at each of the subject's decisions the situation exactly as the subject was told it, its estimated action frequencies against the equilibrium and, for played hands, its raw replies; and at each opponent decision the equilibrium mix, the best response and its per-visit edge. Hands come from three sources, always labelled:

  • Biggest gain (constructed): for each seat, the three infostates with the largest contributions. The hand is the history in that infostate where switching to the best response gains most (reach-weighted), followed by the best response and the subject's most likely actions.
  • Played: the first 100 hands of a play-mode match, with every attempt the subject made.
  • Simulated, only when no play-mode match exists: 100 hands drawn from σ̂ against the equilibrium. Every query is independent and context-free, so these follow the same distribution as live play, but they were not played live.

Explanations are templated. Every sentence on the site is filled in from computed numbers by fixed templates in report/explain.py; none is written by a language model.

Every number traces to a file. Each number on a results page is rendered from a result file published next to the page (runs/<id>/data/*.json), and its HTML element names that file and the JSON pointer of the value. At the end of every build, the site is re-read and each number is checked against its source; the build fails on any mismatch. build.json records the SHA-256 of every published file, and the check also confirms each published file is unchanged. Raw call logs stay in the run directory. Numbers in this document are produced by the commands cited next to them.

Derived files. The breakdown and the replay hands are written into the run directory as breakdown.json and replay.json, computed only from the run's own result files. Each records the SHA-256 of its inputs and is recomputed when they change.

Limitations (so far)

  • Small games. Kuhn poker has 12 infostates, Leduc 288 and Liar's Dice 192 (half of them forced). The poker equilibria are well known and may appear in model training data.
  • Liar's Dice is OpenSpiel's variant (three-sided dice, the highest face wild, a single round), not the common game with 1s wild, so a model that falls back on the usual rules plays a different game. The prompt states the rules in full.
  • Results depend on the prompt wording and on the sampling settings a provider allows. Some current models do not allow the temperature to be changed.
  • The strict parser may count verbose but sensible replies as failures. This is deliberate and is reported through M4.
  • All metrics are measured against a CFR+ reference that is close to, but not exactly, an equilibrium (its own exploitability is shown on the leaderboard). Values smaller than that reference exploitability are not meaningful.
  • The breakdown attributes the gain to the best responder's decisions, not to the subject's mistakes. A leak at one of the subject's decisions usually shows up as gains at the opponent decisions that follow it, which the replay view puts side by side.
